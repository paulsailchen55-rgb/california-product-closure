A product lifecycle should include its closure.

When an ordinary resident is holding the actual object, can the system still tell them what to do with it—legally, affordably, practically, and with enough accountability to know what happens next?

01 · THE QUESTION

Does the system remain usable at the moment a person is holding the object?

This proposal does not assume that California lacks waste management. It asks whether responsibility, information, access, capacity, and downstream accountability remain connected strongly enough to make lawful closure a realistic option.

The goal is not to make every product recyclable. The goal is to make responsible closure a realistic choice.

“
Identification is not disposition.
Collection is not closure.
Movement is not closure.

A material code, label, barcode, donation, transfer, or haul-away can tell us something happened. None of those facts, by themselves, prove that the lifecycle has responsibly closed.

03 · POLICY FRAMEWORK

Ten elements for proportional end-of-life responsibility.

01Identify responsibility

Manufacturer, brand owner, importer, or other responsible producer.

02Identify the pathway

Repair, reuse, take-back, recycling, recovery, or lawful disposal.

03Make access practical

Geographically reasonable access through appropriate partners.

04Make funding proportional

Producer funding or comparable mechanisms so costs are not unexpectedly shifted to the final owner.

05Preserve reuse

Repair, resale, donation, refurbishment, and legitimate second-life pathways count as outcomes.

06Address orphan products

Create a mechanism when the original producer has disappeared or cannot reasonably provide service.

07Include Internet sales

Products sold into California electronically should not escape applicable responsibility.

08Protect small businesses

Use shared producer-funded systems rather than making small retailers operate specialized facilities.

09Measure results

Track access, reuse, recycling, disposal, costs, dumping, environmental outcomes, and economic effects.

10Allow evolution

Add product categories transparently when evidence identifies significant end-of-life gaps.

THE SIX-QUESTION CONSUMER TEST

If a system cannot answer questions 5 and 6, the product lifecycle is incomplete.

  1. Who made it?
  2. Who sold it?
  3. What is it made of?
  4. Where can I repair or reuse it?
  5. Where can I return it?
  6. Who pays for what happens next?

04 · TEST CASE

The WaveStorm question.

One ordinary object can expose a statewide systems question.

A used soft-top surfboard may remain useful for years. The immediate answer may be repair, reuse, donation, or resale. But eventually the board can become a composite foam-and-plastic object with no obvious local endpoint.

The point is not that one surfboard requires a special law. The point is that a single object can expose the general question: why can California make it easy to buy an object without making its eventual destination equally legible?

ALREADY IN MOTION

California is not starting from zero.

The proposal is an extension question, not a claim that existing programs are absent. California already operates multiple producer-responsibility and product-stewardship systems.

Electronic wasteBatteriesPaintMattressesCarpetTextilesPackagingBeverage containersPharmaceuticals & sharps

The policy opportunity is to retain specialized programs where they work, establish a general process for identifying uncovered end-of-life gaps, and create a transparent pathway for adding products when evidence supports intervention.

05 · FEDERAL COMPANION

When the object crosses a boundary, does the responsibility move with it?

The California question is local and practical: can the resident close the lifecycle? The federal companion asks whether information and responsibility remain connected to the product as it crosses county, state, or national boundaries.

When material crosses a jurisdictional boundary, does the information and responsibility necessary to manage it remain attached to the material or product record—or does only the material move?

This is deliberately a separate research question. It should be tested empirically rather than assumed.

THE CLOSURE PRINCIPLE

A responsible system does not stop at production or first use. It includes closure.

We are not looking for the single solution. We are looking for the smallest combination of interventions that closes the identified failure without creating a larger failure elsewhere.
