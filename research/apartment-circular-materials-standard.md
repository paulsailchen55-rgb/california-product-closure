# Apartment Circular Materials Standard

## Comprehensive Research and Pilot Proposal

**Working status:** Conceptual research and design proposal  
**Relationship to repository:** A focused application of the California Product Closure project to apartment living, local material production, household goods, and community recovery systems.

---

## 1. Purpose

Develop an apartment and community materials system that protects and preserves life, freedom, and the conditions that allow people and nature to flourish.

The home should support people rather than become their primary obligation. Household products should help residents live, rest, create, recover, and care for one another. Materials should be designed with their entire life cycle in mind: growing or sourcing, manufacturing, distribution, use, repair, reuse, recovery, and final disposition.

### Guiding principle

> Design homes and everyday necessities to cooperate with natural cycles while leaving people free to serve, create, rest, and care for one another.

### Supporting principles

- Protect and preserve life, health, safety, freedom, and ecological function.
- Prefer renewable or responsibly sourced materials where performance permits.
- Minimize unnecessary material mixing.
- Make products repairable, replaceable, reusable, and understandable.
- Design the end of life before manufacturing begins.
- Avoid shifting burdens from residents to workers, waste handlers, or future generations.
- Treat uncertainty honestly and distinguish demonstrated performance from proposals or hypotheses.
- Use local production where technically, economically, and ecologically appropriate.
- Build emergency usefulness without making maintenance and consumption the purpose of life.

## 2. The Three Material Systems

The project currently considers three connected material systems rather than assuming that every object can be made from exactly three ingredients.

### 2.1 Plant-fiber system

Potential inputs include paper fibers, agricultural residues, hemp, flax, other suitable fibers, and molded-fiber feedstocks.

Potential uses:

- Paper and cardboard containers
- Packaging
- Storage products
- Liners where moisture and contamination requirements permit
- Textiles and cordage
- Panels and nonstructural components

Questions:

- Can the feedstock be grown or collected locally?
- What land, water, energy, and processing equipment are required?
- Does the product require a coating, adhesive, resin, or plastic layer?
- Can the product be reused, recycled, composted, or otherwise recovered?
- What happens when it becomes wet, moldy, greasy, or chemically contaminated?

### 2.2 Structural plant-material system

Potential inputs include wood, bamboo, cork, and other appropriate plant-derived materials.

Potential uses:

- Furniture frames
- Shelving
- Bed structures
- Durable handles and components
- Repairable building and household elements

Questions:

- Is the material suitable for the intended load, moisture, fire, and durability conditions?
- Can components be disassembled and replaced?
- Are preservatives, coatings, laminates, or adhesives needed?
- Can local production avoid ecological damage or unsustainable harvesting?

### 2.3 Binding, joining, and interface system

A third system may be needed for binders, adhesives, fasteners, seals, coatings, or flexible interfaces.

The project should not assume that a binder is harmless because its source is plant-based. Each binder or joining method must be assessed for:

- Chemical composition
- Indoor-air and skin-contact considerations
- Food-contact suitability where relevant
- Water and heat resistance
- Fire behavior
- Repairability
- Separation and end-of-life compatibility
- Worker and resident exposure

Metal hardware, silicone, rubber, or other materials may be permitted as limited exceptions when they provide necessary safety, sealing, durability, or repair functions. The objective is material simplicity and responsible closure, not an unsupported claim that every product can be entirely plant-based.

## 3. Three Sisters and Ecological Design Inspiration

The Three Sisters agricultural system—commonly associated with corn, beans, and squash among multiple Indigenous nations—offers an inspiration for thinking about relationships between parts of a living system. Specific traditions, meanings, and practices differ among nations and should not be generalized into one universal account.

The project draws only a limited design analogy:

- Different components can support one another.
- A system can produce multiple useful outcomes.
- Maintenance and reciprocity matter.
- Diversity can improve resilience under changing conditions.
- Materials and resources should be used with respect and responsibility.

This project should credit Indigenous knowledge accurately and avoid presenting a modern housing system as equivalent to the traditions from which the inspiration comes.

## 5. Reusable Containers and Liner Strategy

A core product concept is a durable container with a replaceable or washable liner.

The container would be designed for repeated use. The liner would handle specific conditions such as moisture, food residue, dirt, or contamination when the main structure cannot safely do so.

Design requirements:

- Clearly identify the container and liner materials.
- Avoid hidden multilayer combinations where possible.
- Make liners easy to replace without discarding the container.
- State whether the liner is reusable, recyclable, compostable, or disposable.
- Specify the actual collection or disposal route.
- Prevent false claims that a liner is safe in all environments.
- Test odor, mold, leakage, cleaning, and durability performance.

A liner should be selected for the actual use case. Paper, plant fiber, silicone, natural rubber, or another material may be appropriate in different situations. Reusability must be evaluated over the full life cycle, including cleaning, transport, energy use, and eventual disposal.

## 6. Richlite and Similar Composites

Richlite is a paper-based composite that uses phenolic resin. It may be useful for durable surfaces and some furniture applications, but it should not be described as plastic-free, fully biodegradable, or equivalent to untreated paper.

The project should maintain a material evidence record for every candidate product:

- Ingredients and binders
- Coatings and additives
- Food-contact or other relevant certifications
- Expected service life
- Repair options
- Reuse options
- Recycling or recovery options
- Contamination limitations
- Final disposal conditions

A candidate material should not be accepted solely because it contains plant fibers.

## 7. Local Growing and Production

The long-term vision is to grow or source material within apartments, apartment buildings, or nearby facilities designed for local production.

Possible infrastructure includes:

- Shared plant-growing spaces
- Fiber-processing rooms or regional facilities
- Community workshops
- Repair and replacement stations
- Local storage and distribution
- Material libraries and demonstration areas

Feasibility questions:

- Which plants can be grown in the local climate and available space?
- What are the water, soil, lighting, and energy requirements?
- Can growing operations avoid mold and indoor-air problems?
- What processing steps require industrial equipment?
- What materials must still be imported?
- What is the cost per useful product compared with conventional supply chains?
- Does local production genuinely reduce total environmental burden?

Local production should be treated as a design goal to test, not an automatic environmental benefit.

## 8. Mold, Moisture, and Contamination

Plant-based materials can be vulnerable to moisture, mold, pests, staining, and biological degradation. A circular system must plan for these risks instead of assuming that natural decomposition is always beneficial indoors.

Requirements to investigate:

- Moisture management
- Ventilation
- Drying and cleaning
- Mold prevention and remediation
- Safe separation of contaminated materials
- Fire behavior
- Pest control
- Resident notification and replacement
- Worker protection during handling and processing

When a material becomes contaminated, the correct outcome may be reuse, controlled treatment, specialized disposal, or another route—not automatic composting or grinding.

## 9. Recovery and Processing Technology

The project includes a future concept for a sealed, community-scale recovery unit. The initial concept should not be described as a proven dust-making machine.

### Processes that must be distinguished

- Mechanical size reduction: making pieces smaller
- Separation: dividing materials into distinct streams
- Granulation: producing particles of controlled size
- Chemical recycling: converting materials into usable chemical feedstocks
- Biodegradation: biological decomposition under suitable conditions
- Mineral recovery: extracting specific elements or compounds

Abrasive blasting or grinding can create hazardous dust, noise, heat, and contaminated mixtures. Turning a material into dust does not prove that it has returned to harmless earth components. Richlite, silicone, coatings, adhesives, and contaminated household products require separate technical assessment.

### Conceptual safe-processing sequence

1. Inspect and identify incoming material.
2. Reject unknown, hazardous, or unsuitable inputs.
3. Use a process appropriate to the specific material.
4. Keep processing enclosed and controlled.
5. Capture and monitor dust and emissions.
6. Control noise, fire, heat, and mechanical hazards.
7. Separate and test output streams.
8. Direct recovered material to a verified use.
9. Manage residual waste through an appropriate permitted pathway.
10. Maintain records of inputs, outputs, incidents, and maintenance.

The design goal is that residents do not need to perform industrial blasting or handle hazardous dust. Trained personnel and appropriate engineering controls must be responsible for industrial processing.

## 10. Regulatory and Safety Questions

The proposal must be reviewed against applicable federal, California, county, and city requirements before any installation or pilot.

Questions for agencies and technical experts:

- What solid-waste permits or authorizations would apply?
- Would the operation be classified as recycling, processing, treatment, or another category?
- What air-quality, dust, noise, fire, and worker-safety requirements apply?
- What building, electrical, mechanical, and accessibility requirements apply?
- How would hazardous or contaminated materials be identified and rejected?
- What monitoring, recordkeeping, emergency shutdown, and inspection requirements are necessary?
- Who is responsible for operation, maintenance, and residual waste?
- What evidence would be required before operation in a residential building?

The fact that a system is not an incinerator does not, by itself, establish that it is permitted or safe.

## 11. Circular Service and Economic Model

The system could be organized as a product-and-service model rather than a one-time sale of objects.

Possible functions:

- Standardized products and components
- Mail-order replacement parts
- Local repair services
- Return programs for damaged items
- Shared tools and processing facilities
- Material take-back programs
- Reuse markets
- Cooperative or commercial manufacturing
- Public documentation and open designs

The system should track whether the service is practically accessible to ordinary residents. A website or promise is not sufficient if the resident lacks transportation, money, collection access, information, or a functioning physical pathway.

## 12. Pilot Proposal

A first pilot should use a limited set of clean, known materials rather than mixed household waste.

### Phase 1: Research and specification

- Select a small number of candidate materials.
- Document composition, safety, durability, and end-of-life conditions.
- Identify local growing and production possibilities.
- Identify technical and regulatory partners.

### Phase 2: Product prototypes

- Develop containers, storage products, and replaceable liners.
- Test moisture, cleaning, load, repair, and repeated-use performance.
- Record failures and redesign requirements.

### Phase 3: Collection and return test

- Establish clear collection categories.
- Test whether residents can understand and use the system.
- Measure contamination, participation, access, and cost.

### Phase 4: Controlled recovery test

- Use an appropriately equipped and permitted facility.
- Process only approved material streams.
- Measure dust, noise, energy use, output quality, residual waste, and worker exposure controls.

### Phase 5: Evaluation

Evaluate:

- Resident usability
- Safety
- Total cost
- Material recovery rate
- Repair and replacement time
- Mold and contamination incidents
- Worker conditions
- Environmental tradeoffs
- Reliability during ordinary and emergency conditions

Do not scale the system based only on enthusiasm or a successful demonstration. Scale only after evidence supports the intended use.

## 14. Resident Outcome Test

The project should repeatedly ask:

> Could an ordinary resident actually do something differently, more easily, more safely, or more reliably than before?

Possible outcome labels:

- Demonstrated
- Reported but not independently verified
- Partial improvement
- No demonstrated change
- Unknown

The project should measure practical access rather than merely counting products, webpages, recommendations, or facilities.

## 15. Public Explanation

A concise public description could read:

> The Apartment Circular Materials Standard is a research and design project exploring locally renewable materials, repairable household products, replaceable components, and responsible material recovery. It aims to help homes cooperate with natural cycles while preserving residents' time, freedom, safety, and ability to care for one another. The project distinguishes established evidence from experimental concepts and treats the full life cycle of each material as part of its design.

### Core statement

> Protect and preserve life, freedom, and the conditions that allow communities and nature to flourish.

## 16. Open Questions

1. What are the most suitable three material systems for the local climate and intended products?
2. Which plants can realistically be grown or processed locally?
3. Which household products can safely use fiber-based structures?
4. Where are silicone, rubber, metal, or other materials necessary?
5. Which liners are reusable, recyclable, compostable, or safely disposable under actual local conditions?
6. How can mold and contamination be detected and managed?
7. What recovery technologies can process each material without creating a new hazard?
8. What regulatory pathway would apply to a community-scale pilot?

## Status and Limitations

This is a conceptual research and pilot proposal. It is not a completed engineering design, safety certification, regulatory determination, product certification, or business plan. Claims about materials, health, environmental performance, local production, and recovery technology require source-based research and testing before public or commercial implementation.
