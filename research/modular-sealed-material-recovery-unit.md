# Modular Sealed Material-Recovery Unit

## Concept specification — research and design basis

**Status:** Conceptual research / preliminary design specification. Not an engineering design, safety certification, regulatory determination, or approved household appliance.

## 1. Purpose

Develop a modular, non-incinerating material-recovery unit intended for an apartment building, community facility, or potentially a residence. The unit is designed around the same modularity principle as the products it receives: furniture and household goods should be intentionally separable into identifiable components before entering the machine.

The purpose is not to make waste disappear. The purpose is to reduce, separate, contain, identify, and route materials toward reuse, remanufacture, biological processing, mineral recovery, or lawful residual disposal without transferring uncontrolled dust or contamination to residents, workers, soil, air, or water.

## 2. Core design idea

Think of the unit as a **Lego-like processing module**:

- The machine itself is one standardized module.
- Furniture and household products are designed as modules that can be opened and separated.
- Components enter through a controlled loading interface.
- Internal robotic handling keeps people outside the processing enclosure.
- Size reduction is performed only as necessary for separation or recovery.
- Materials are separated into controlled streams rather than intentionally reduced to "nothingness."
- Each recovered stream is enclosed, labeled, and directed to an appropriate next step.
- The machine can be installed as one unit or connected to a larger building/community recovery system.

## 3. Important correction to the original "blast it into dust" idea

Abrasive blasting, grinding, crushing, or pulverizing a mixed household object can create respirable dust, heat, noise, sparks, fragments, and contaminated mixtures. Dust is not automatically harmless just because it is mineral or because the original object was considered ordinary household furniture.

Therefore the engineering target should be:

> **Controlled material liberation and separation, not destruction into dust.**

Abrasive or other high-energy processing can remain a possible research pathway, but it must be treated as a contained industrial process requiring hazard controls, filtration, monitoring, maintenance procedures, and material-specific validation.

Where a component can be removed intact, that is preferable to pulverizing it.

## 4. Conceptual physical architecture

### 4.1 Outer enclosure

The unit is envisioned as a sealed cabinet or vessel approximately analogous to a large commercial refuse container, but engineered specifically for controlled processing.

Required conceptual zones:

1. **Input/load zone** — accepts only approved, identified modules.
2. **Isolation chamber** — prevents direct airflow and material escape when loading/unloading.
3. **Processing chamber** — sealed during active operation.
4. **Robotic handling zone** — one or more articulated arms manipulate components.
5. **Separation zone** — screens, vibration, air classification, magnetic separation, optical sensing, or other appropriate methods may be evaluated.
6. **Output containment zone** — recovered streams are transferred to closed containers.
7. **Residual zone** — material that cannot be safely identified or recovered is isolated rather than mixed into a useful stream.

The enclosure should operate under controlled airflow so that any leakage tendency is inward rather than outward, subject to engineering validation.

## 5. Robotic handling system

The initial concept uses two or more internal robotic arms.

### Arm A — positioning / inspection

Possible functions:

- grasp a component;
- rotate it;
- hold it against a processing fixture;
- present different surfaces to sensors;
- separate attached modules;
- place material into a designated output stream.

### Arm B — processing assistance

Possible functions:

- stabilize a component during size reduction;
- manipulate material around a fixed processing head;
- present surfaces to an abrasive, cutting, shredding, milling, or other tool;
- move liberated material to a separation stage.

### Alternative architecture

The processing head does not necessarily need to be mounted on an arm. A fixed processing station may be safer and simpler, with robotic arms moving the material through it.

This should be tested against:

- containment;
- maintenance complexity;
- energy consumption;
- failure modes;
- tool wear;
- throughput;
- ability to prevent jams;
- worker exposure during servicing.

The design should not assume that more robotic movement is automatically better.

## 6. Processing principle

The preferred sequence is:

**Identify → disassemble → inspect → isolate hazards → separate intact components → reduce size only when necessary → separate material families → verify → contain → route.**

A possible processing train could include:

1. Mechanical fastener removal.
2. Controlled cutting or delamination where appropriate.
3. Low-energy shredding or granulation for selected plant-fiber components.
4. Screening by particle size.
5. Controlled vibration to stratify or move material.
6. Magnetic separation for ferrous metals where applicable.
7. Density or air classification where appropriate and safely contained.
8. Optical or other sensing for material identification.
9. Separate collection containers for validated material streams.
10. Residual isolation for unknown or contaminated material.

The system should be designed so that every processing step has a reason. A material should not be pulverized merely because the machine can pulverize it.

## 7. Vibration and "layer separation"

The original concept includes vibrating the processed material so that different components settle into layers and can be collected as blocks.

This is a useful hypothesis to test, but it should not yet be treated as a physical fact. Mixed materials do not necessarily separate cleanly into layers simply through vibration.

Research should evaluate combinations of:

- particle size;
- density;
- shape;
- moisture content;
- electrostatic behavior;
- magnetic properties;
- air-flow behavior;
- surface properties.

A vibrating screen/deck may be useful, but the actual separation mechanism should be selected after material testing.

## 8. Output blocks

The concept calls for recovered material to be packaged into compact, stackable blocks.

This is potentially useful for logistics, but the block must not become another permanent mixed-material waste product.

### Preferred output rule

> **One block = one known, controlled material stream, unless a documented downstream process is specifically designed for the mixture.**

Each block/container should have a machine-readable and human-readable identity containing, where feasible:

- material family;
- approximate composition;
- source batch;
- contamination status;
- moisture status;
- processing history;
- intended next destination;
- handling warnings;
- date/time of production.

## 9. The proposed biological/mineral "skin"

The user concept describes an outer microbial compaction that seals the recovered dust inside a transportable block.

This should remain a **research hypothesis**, not a specified technology. A living microbial layer may introduce its own biological, moisture, odor, contamination, stability, and regulatory questions.

Potential alternatives to investigate include:

- molded fiber shells;
- mineral-based binders where appropriate;
- compostable binders for compatible organic streams;
- reusable rigid containers;
- sealed replaceable liners;
- mechanically closed containers that do not require a permanent binder.

A key design test is whether the package can be opened at the next processing destination without creating an uncontrolled exposure event.

## 10. Modular output container

The output package should behave like a standardized shipping module.

Conceptual requirements:

- stackable;
- stable during transport;
- closed against dust leakage;
- resistant to expected moisture conditions;
- easy to inspect;
- easy to identify;
- compatible with mechanical handling;
- openable at a controlled receiving facility;
- reusable when practical;
- recyclable/compostable only when its actual composition and contamination status permit it.

The container is part of the circular system and therefore needs its own end-of-life pathway.

## 11. Product-to-machine compatibility

The machine works best if the furniture is designed for it from the beginning.

Examples of modular construction rules:

- mechanical fasteners instead of permanent mixed-material bonding where practical;
- visible or standardized fastener access;
- replaceable panels;
- replaceable upholstery or liners;
- separable wood/plant-fiber/metal interfaces;
- standardized component dimensions;
- labels or markings identifying material families;
- minimal unnecessary material combinations;
- no hidden hazardous treatments;
- documented disassembly order;
- components sized so that one person or a safe mechanical aid can handle them.

A chair, table, cabinet, bed, or storage unit should be thought of as a collection of modules rather than a single indivisible object.

## 12. Household and apartment-scale versions

The same architecture could exist at several scales:

### Level 1 — household preparation

Residents separate approved modular components and place them into clearly identified collection modules.

### Level 2 — apartment building unit

A shared sealed unit processes compatible material streams for multiple households.

### Level 3 — neighborhood/community unit

A larger unit handles materials requiring more specialized equipment and controls.

### Level 4 — regional receiving facility

Specialized facilities handle hazardous, contaminated, unusual, or economically valuable materials that should not be processed in a home or ordinary apartment building.

This hierarchy prevents the household machine from becoming an attempt to process everything.

## 13. Safety architecture

Safety is a primary design requirement, not an accessory.

The machine should be designed around the assumption that household objects can contain unknown materials.

Potential hazards include:

- respirable dust;
- toxic dust or fibers;
- mold or biological contamination;
- batteries and stored electrical energy;
- pressurized containers;
- chemicals;
- treated wood;
- flame-retardant materials;
- sharp fragments;
- moving machinery;
- pinch/crush points;
- sparks and ignition sources;
- heat;
- noise;
- vibration;
- unexpected material failure.

### Minimum conceptual safeguards

- interlocked access doors;
- controlled loading and unloading;
- emergency stop systems;
- fire detection/suppression appropriate to the validated process;
- dust capture and filtration;
- pressure/airflow monitoring;
- temperature monitoring;
- vibration monitoring;
- jam detection;
- tool-wear monitoring;
- automatic shutdown on unsafe conditions;
- safe maintenance isolation/lockout procedures;
- inspection access without exposing workers to accumulated dust;
- documented prohibited inputs.

The unit should never be marketed as safe merely because it is enclosed.

## 14. Material acceptance gate

Before processing, the system should answer:

1. What is this object?
2. What materials are present?
3. Is it permitted in this machine?
4. Is it contaminated?
5. Does it contain a battery or other stored energy?
6. Does it require specialized handling?
7. Can it be reused or repaired instead of processed?

If those questions cannot be answered sufficiently, the machine should route the object to a quarantine/rejection pathway.

## 15. Recovery hierarchy

The machine should follow a hierarchy rather than maximizing throughput:

1. **Keep the object in use.**
2. **Repair it.**
3. **Replace a component.**
4. **Disassemble for reuse.**
5. **Recover a clean material stream.**
6. **Process a compatible material into a new feedstock.**
7. **Contain and route unavoidable residuals to an appropriate permitted facility.**

This keeps the machine from creating a market incentive to destroy useful products merely to feed the machine.

## 16. Energy principle

The unit should be evaluated on total energy and material cost, not only whether it avoids combustion.

Measurements should include:

- electricity per kilogram processed;
- electricity per recovered kilogram;
- embodied energy of consumable tooling;
- filter replacement;
- container production;
- maintenance;
- transport avoided or added;
- percentage of material actually recovered;
- residual waste generated.

A non-incinerator is not automatically environmentally preferable. The entire lifecycle must be measured.

## 17. Noise and vibration

Because the proposed system may use abrasive, cutting, grinding, shredding, and vibration processes, noise and structural vibration are design constraints.

For an apartment installation, the machine should be evaluated for:

- airborne noise;
- structure-borne vibration;
- nighttime operation;
- effects on adjacent units;
- maintenance noise;
- emergency shutdown noise;
- foundation/isolation requirements.

A household-scale version may ultimately require much lower-energy processing methods than an industrial version.

## 18. Data and traceability

The machine should maintain a simple material record without turning recordkeeping into another burden.

A batch record could contain only the information needed to answer:

> Where did this material come from, what happened to it, what is it now, and where can it safely go next?

This supports accountability while preserving the user's broader principle of minimizing administrative friction.

## 19. Modularity of the machine itself

The machine should be assembled from replaceable modules.

Potential modules include:

- loading module;
- isolation/airlock module;
- robotic handling module;
- inspection/sensing module;
- cutting module;
- abrasive processing module;
- granulation module;
- vibrating-screen module;
- magnetic separation module;
- air-classification module;
- filtration module;
- output-container module;
- controls/safety module;
- power module;
- fire-control module.

A facility should not have to replace the entire machine when one processing function becomes obsolete or needs repair.

## 20. "Lego" interface standard

The long-term research opportunity is to define a common interface between machine modules.

Each module could have standardized:

- physical mounting points;
- power connection;
- data connection;
- emergency-stop interface;
- airflow interface where needed;
- material inlet/outlet dimensions;
- identification;
- maintenance access;
- safety interlocks.

The same philosophy can be applied to furniture components.

This creates two related standards:

**Product modularity:** things can be separated.

**Machine modularity:** the equipment can be repaired, upgraded, and reconfigured.

## 21. Research questions before construction

The following should be answered experimentally and through engineering review:

1. Which household materials can be safely processed at apartment scale?
2. Which materials should never enter the unit?
3. Can products be designed so most components are removable without high-energy processing?
4. Which separation methods work for each material family?
5. What particle sizes are actually useful for downstream recovery?
6. How can dust exposure be measured and controlled?
7. What filtration technology is appropriate for each validated material?
8. Can vibration improve separation without creating excessive airborne dust?
9. What container geometry provides safe stacking and transport?
10. Can containers be reused rather than permanently sealed?
11. What does a microbial or compostable shell actually require, and is it necessary?
12. What happens when the machine loses power during processing?
13. What happens when a robot arm fails while holding material?
14. How is a jam cleared without exposing a worker?
15. How are batteries, chemicals, mold, asbestos-containing materials, and other special hazards excluded or routed?
16. What building code, fire code, environmental, occupational-safety, waste, and electrical requirements apply at each installation scale?
17. What level of maintenance can a normal building actually support?
18. What is the lifecycle cost per recovered material stream?
19. What percentage of incoming material remains residual waste?
20. What is the safest useful minimum machine rather than the largest possible machine?

## 22. Proposed development stages

### Stage A — paper architecture

Define material classes, modular product interfaces, machine modules, acceptance rules, outputs, and safety boundaries.

### Stage B — non-hazardous bench experiments

Test simple clean materials using small, controlled separation equipment. Do not use unknown household waste.

### Stage C — sealed prototype research

Build a professionally engineered enclosure and test controlled material flows with appropriate industrial hygiene and safety review.

### Stage D — modular machine demonstration

Demonstrate replaceable processing modules and standardized material containers.

### Stage E — institutional pilot

Test in a controlled facility before considering residential deployment.

### Stage F — apartment-scale pilot

Only after hazards, noise, fire safety, maintenance, emissions, and regulatory requirements are demonstrated to be manageable.

### Stage G — household version, if justified

A home unit should be smaller and more conservative than an industrial system. It should reject most materials that require specialized controls.

## 23. Success criteria

A successful system should demonstrate all of the following:

- people are not required to enter the processing chamber during ordinary operation;
- the machine does not rely on combustion;
- material streams remain identifiable;
- useful products are preserved whenever practical;
- dust and other emissions are controlled and measured;
- hazardous inputs are rejected or isolated;
- outputs have a real downstream destination;
- containers do not become an undocumented permanent waste sink;
- modules can be repaired/replaced;
- energy and maintenance costs are measured;
- residents can understand what goes in and what comes out;
- the system reduces rather than relocates environmental and labor burdens.

## 24. Relationship to the apartment circular-materials standard

This machine is the **recovery/closure component** of the larger apartment materials concept.

The upstream standard establishes modular products and limited material families.

The machine provides a possible downstream pathway for components that are no longer usable.

Together they form a loop:

**Grow / source → manufacture → use → repair → reuse → disassemble → recover → remake / return → residual handling.**

The machine therefore should not be designed first. The product standard, material specification, and end-of-life pathways must be designed together.

## 25. Design principle

> **Build things so they can come apart before we build machines that have to tear them apart.**

And for the machine itself:

> **Build the recovery machine so it can come apart, be repaired, and be upgraded without becoming another waste problem.**

The larger objective remains:

> **Make a home that cooperates with nature's cycle while leaving people free to serve, create, rest, and care for one another — to protect and preserve.**

## 26. Current status

This document captures the user's conceptual machine architecture and converts it into a researchable engineering direction. It deliberately does **not** claim that abrasive blasting can safely reduce mixed household furniture to harmless dust, that vibration will automatically produce clean layers, or that a microbial shell is currently a validated packaging technology.

Those are hypotheses for investigation.

The immediate next task is to create a **system architecture diagram and requirements matrix** showing the material flow, machine modules, safety boundaries, product interfaces, and output pathways without prematurely specifying unsafe machinery parameters.
